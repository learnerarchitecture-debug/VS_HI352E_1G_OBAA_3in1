# Topic 1 — Adaptive Reuse of Abandoned Buildings into Mixed-Use Hubs

## 1.1 Case Studies (built projects)

### Tate Modern — London, UK
- Original → new: Bankside Power Station (1952, oil-fired; decommissioned 1981) → contemporary art museum
- Architect: Herzog & de Meuron (original: Giles Gilbert Scott); Switch House extension 2016
- Design strategies: light-touch conversion; retained turbine hall as public "street"; galleries of 5–12 m height; illuminated glass roof beam as only major exterior addition; perforated brick extension
- Scale: 34,000 m²; world's most-visited modern art museum; catalyst for Bankside regeneration
- Sources: https://www.archdaily.com/429700/ad-classics-the-tate-modern-herzog-and-de-meuron · https://www.herzogdemeuron.com/recordings/126-tate-modern-263-the-tate-modern-project/

### Battersea Power Station — London, UK
- Original → new: 1930s–50s coal-fired power station (decommissioned 1983) → mixed-use: retail, 254 apartments, offices, leisure
- Architects: WilkinsonEyre, Foster + Partners, Gehry Partners, Purcell (masterplan Rafael Viñoly); opened 2022
- Strategies: retained brick shell and four chimneys; turbine halls → retail/public space; residential blocks inserted within; 42-acre masterplan as district catalyst
- Scale: 42 acres; ~6.2 million sq ft planned
- Sources: https://batterseapowerstation.co.uk/about/building-battersea-the-masterplan/

### CaixaForum Madrid — Madrid, Spain
- Original → new: Central Eléctrica del Mediodía (1899–1901 brick power plant) → art museum + cultural centre (2008)
- Architect: Herzog & de Meuron
- Strategies: dramatic subtraction — building visually "floated" above a new public plaza; retained brick façades; stacked rusted-steel volumes; roof garden
- Scale: ~10,000 m²
- Source: https://www.herzogdemeuron.com/projects/201-caixaforum-madrid/

### Zeitz MOCAA — Cape Town, South Africa
- Original → new: 1920s grain silos (42 concrete silos, decommissioned 1990s) → Zeitz Museum of Contemporary Art Africa (2017)
- Architect: Heatherwick Studio (with Van der Merwe Miszewski)
- Strategies: interior "excavated" into a cathedral-like atrium; concrete tubes retained as structure; sculptural circulation; historic exterior preserved
- Scale: 9,500 m² exhibition space, 80+ galleries
- Source: https://heatherwick.com/project/zeitz-mocaa/

### Fondaco dei Tedeschi — Venice, Italy
- Original → new: 13th-century merchants' trading post (rebuilt 1505–08; post office; monument-listed 1987) → department store with public roof terrace (2017)
- Architect: OMA (Rem Koolhaas et al.)
- Strategies: "finite number of strategic interventions" — escalator core, steel-and-glass floor over the courtyard, restored rooftop terrace opened to the public; courtyard as covered urban piazza; anti-nostalgic, layered-history approach
- Note: DFS store closed May 2025 — instructive case of programmatic failure after architectural success
- Sources: https://www.archdaily.com/789167/il-fondaco-dei-tedeschi-oma · closure: https://www.designboom.com/architecture/oma-fondaco-dei-tedeschi-store-venice-doors-dfs-05-02-2025

### The High Line — New York City, USA
- Original → new: 1930s elevated freight railway (abandoned 1980) → 2.33 km elevated public park (2009/2011/2014–19)
- Designers: James Corner Field Operations, Diller Scofidio + Renfro, Piet Oudolf
- Strategies: preserved rail structure and planting beds; phased, community-led public-private model; "slow architecture", salvaged steel, re-wilding
- Economics (verified, Landscape Performance Series): $1.4B tax revenue to NYC 2007–2027 (~$65M/yr) vs ~$140M city investment (>900% return); $2B+ catalyzed development; 3,000 net new dwelling units in West Chelsea; +35.3% housing value within 5-min walk (2003–2011, peer-reviewed); ~6M visitors (2015); eco-gentrification critique
- Sources: https://landscapeperformance.org/case-study-briefs/high-line · https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0169204619314574

### High Street Phoenix / Phoenix Mills — Mumbai, India
- Original → new: 1905 cotton textile mill complex (17.3 acres) → retail-led mixed-use hub: High Street Phoenix + Palladium mall, hotel (2012), offices, residences
- Developer: The Phoenix Mills Ltd; transformation since 1980s; India's model of mill-to-mixed-use
- FY2025 figures (Integrated Annual Report 2024-25): 12 malls, 11.5 msf GLA in 8 cities; consumption ₹13,750 crore (+21%); retail rental income ₹1,951 crore (+18%); retail EBITDA ₹2,010 crore; 91% trading occupancy
- Sources: https://www.thephoenixmills.com/InvestorFiles/388fe194-6cd3-4f7e-9e99-dd2289b1c56d.pdf (AR 2024-25)

### N.M. Wadia Building — Mumbai, India (Fort district)
- Neo-classical civic building → retail shops, restaurants, offices; minimal-intervention reuse; part of Fort-area colonial-building conversions (Zara in Ismail Building, Army & Navy Building)
- Sources: https://www.re-thinkingthefuture.com/city-and-architecture/a4476-adaptive-reuse-south-bombay-india/ · https://www.mumbaiheritageproject.org/task-i-ii-new/n.m.-wadia-bldg.

### Haveli Dharampura — Delhi, India (Chandni Chowk)
- Original → new: 1887 Mughal-style haveli (abandoned) → 13–14-room heritage boutique hotel, restaurants, gallery (opened 2016)
- Restorers: Vijay Goel & Siddhant Goel; architect Kapil Agarwal; restoration 2011–2016 (6 yrs, ~50 masons); UNESCO Asia-Pacific Award 2017
- Strategies: traditional lime (chuna) construction; minimum intervention; lakhori brick; private funding, no government support; first haveli fully restored in Delhi in 100 years
- Sources: https://havelidharampura.com/restoration-story.html · https://www.re-thinkingthefuture.com/architectural-styles/a7884-timeline-of-restoration-haveli-dharampura-new-delhi/

### ZIN (WTC Brussels) — Brussels, Belgium
- Original → new: 1970s twin office towers → vertical mixed-use district: offices (~3,900 civil servants), rental apartments, hotel, retail, sports, public green space (completed 2024)
- Architects: 51N4E + Jaspers-Eyers + L'AUC
- Strategies: >60% of existing structure preserved; connecting double-height volume; circular economy — material passports, Cradle-to-Cradle; open ground floor as public address; CTBUH 2025 World's Best Tall Building
- Scale: ~110,000 m² (75,000 m² offices; 14,000 m² flats; 16,000 m² hotel/leisure/retail)
- Source: https://51n4e.com/projects/zin/

### Browary Wrocławskie — Wrocław, Poland
- Original → new: 19th-century brewery → mixed-use district: 831 units, offices, retail, coworking, open-air cinema (2017–2024)
- Architect: SRDK Studio; 3D laser scanning of historic fabric; selective preservation (chimney, bottling tower, lagering hall) with modern insertions; ~40% green space, 36% green roofs; 80% parking underground
- Scale: 3.9 ha; 99,017 m²
- Source: https://srdkstudio.com/en/project/browary-wroclawskie

### Bottleworks District — Indianapolis, USA
- Original → new: Coca-Cola Bottling Plant (1931–1954, world's largest) → food hall, 140-room hotel, cinema, retail, 180,000 sq ft offices (Phase 1 opened Dec 2020)
- Developers/Designers: Hendricks Commercial Properties; RATIO + EUA
- Strategies: Art Deco glazed-terracotta façade preservation; Federal Historic Tax Credits ($78.8M qualified rehab; $129.2M total cost); Indiana Landmarks 2023 Cook Cup
- Sources: https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/coca-cola-bottling-plant.htm · https://urbanland.uli.org/design-planning/project-profile-a-coca-cola-bottling-plant-becomes-an-entertainment-and-cultural-hub

### Norblin Factory — Warsaw, Poland
- Original → new: 19th-century metal-goods factory → 41,000 m² A+ offices, boutique cinema (525 seats), Food Town, BioBazar, museum (2021)
- Developer: Capital Park; architect PRC Architekci; conservation + contemporary infill; two 24/7 internal pedestrian "streets"; BREEAM Excellent; EIB-financed (€60M)
- Scale: 2 ha; >65,000 m²
- Source: https://capitalpark.pl/en/projects/norblin-factory/

### Alembic City / Art District — Vadodara, India
- Original → new: 1907 pharmaceutical campus (100+ acres) → mixed-use township + Art District (museum, studios, restaurants, skate park; museum opened 2019)
- Architects: KGA (Karan Grover & Associates)
- Strategies: "sculptural" conservation of 5 factory buildings — exposed brick, riveted steel trusses, Mangalore tile roofs; 0.45 m load-bearing walls for passive cooling (~22°C interiors); 3,000+ trees preserved; cultural-anchor phasing
- Sources: https://www.re-thinkingthefuture.com/case-studies/a13869-alembic-art-district-vadodara

### Cinnamon Boutique — Bengaluru, India (Ulsoor)
- Original → new: 1892 famine-era orphanage → design boutique + café with courtyard events space (2014)
- Architect: Mathew & Ghosh; structural stabilization, retained flooring/pillars/plasterwork; courtyard as public/exhibit space
- Scale: site 1,500 m²; built ~680 m²
- Source: https://www.mathewghosh.com/adaptive-reuse-cinnamon.html

## 1.3 Research Data & Statistics

- **Vacancy:** US office vacancy 18.6% (Sept 2025, highest on record); India office vacancy 13.85% (C&W Q1 2026), 15.7% (JLL Q3 2025); UK 676,304 empty homes in England (2022); commercial buildings demolished at average age of only 36 years (Moisio et al. 2026)
- **Carbon:** Buildings sector ≈21% of global GHG emissions, ~9.8 Gt CO₂ (2023, GlobalABC); AR = 53–75% lower impacts (Hasik 2019); office-to-residential AR = 54% upfront GHG / 81% lifecycle / 48% cost (Moisio 2026); 20–40% lower whole-life carbon (INREV/Jamestown 2025); renovation typically 50–75% less embodied carbon (AIA); RMI/Skanska 2021: 19–46% upfront EC cuts at <1% cost premium; foundations/slabs/framing = 40–60% of new-build embodied carbon; C&D debris ≈30–35% of landfill waste by weight
- **Economics:** High Line $1.4B tax revenue vs ~$140M investment (>900% return); Phoenix Mills FY2025 consumption ₹13,750 crore (+21%); Bottleworks $78.8M qualified rehab, $129.2M total; vendor data: avg 40% cost savings across 340 US conversions, AR starts +52% YoY 2025 `[verify against primary sources]`
- **India:** Mumbai mill-district conversions; Haveli Dharampura 100% privately funded; UNESCO Asia-Pacific Award 2017; vendor market-size figures `[unverified — do not cite]`

## 1.4 Debates & Gaps

1. **Carbon accounting:** results diverge by scope (A1–A3 vs whole-life), functional unit, demolition inclusion — need harmonized LCA/LCC baselines
2. **Cost paradox:** RMI shows 46% cheaper; conversion-specific costs (abatement, services) can erase the advantage
3. **Eco-gentrification:** High Line +35.3% uplift celebrated vs displacement risk; little work on AR for affordable housing at scale
4. **Authenticity vs viability:** Haveli Dharampura pivoted museum→hotel; Fondaco closed after ~8 years; AR under-theorized (Lanz & Pendlebury)
5. **India-specific gaps:** regulatory friction (ASI/FSI), no fiscal incentives, scarce measured documentation, loss of traditional craft skills (lime masonry), no published POE or LCA for Indian AR projects
6. **Post-COVID conversions:** record vacancy makes office-to-residential urgent but constrained by floor plates, light, zoning (Gursel 2023; ULI 2026)

# Topic 2 — Vertical Village: High-Density Affordable Community Housing

## 2.1 Case Studies (built projects)

### Kampung Admiralty — Singapore (Woodlands)
- Architect: WOHA; completed 2017; 11 storeys; 104 senior two-room "Flexi" flats on ~0.8 ha `[verify]`; Singapore's first integrated development with public facilities under one roof
- Model: HDB subsidised public housing; co-located childcare, medical centre, hawker centre, retail
- Design: layered "club sandwich" section — Community Plaza at grade → Medical Centre → rooftop Community Park under apartments; intergenerational programming; rooftop farm
- Awards: World Building of the Year (WAF 2018); ULI Global Award for Excellence 2020
- Source: https://woha.net/project/kampung-admiralty/

### SkyVille@Dawson — Singapore
- Architect: WOHA (with RSP); 2015; 960 units in three ~47-storey towers; ≈2.7 ha → ≈355 du/ha `[verify]`; HDB BTO public housing
- Design: "streets in the sky" — elevated sky gardens at multiple levels, open corridors for ventilation and chance encounters, ground-level public park
- Source: https://woha.net/project/skyville-dawson/

### Pinnacle@Duxton — Singapore
- Architect: ARC Studio (with RSP); 2009; 1,848 units, 7 towers of 50 storeys; ≈739 du/ha `[verify]`; two 500 m sky bridges at levels 26 and 50 (publicly accessible); DBSS subsidised sale housing; sky gardens and community farming at height
- Source: ARC Studio project pages; ULI case study

### The Interlace — Singapore
- Architect: OMA / Ole Scheeren (with RSP); 2013; 1,040 units; 31 stacked six-storey blocks on 8 ha → ≈130 du/ha; ≈70% of ground open `[verify]`
- Model: private condominium — leading *private* "vertical village"
- Design: offset stacked blocks create communal courtyards, rooftop gardens, pools, "hanging gardens"; village scale in high-rise perimeter
- Award: World Building of the Year 2015
- Sources: https://www.designboom.com/architecture/oma-ole-scheeren-the-interlace-singapore-06-27-2014/ · https://ole-scheeren.com/

### Sky Habitat — Singapore (Bishan)
- Architect: Moshe Safdie with DCA; TOP 27 Apr 2015; **509 units** in two 38-storey towers (verified); >70% of ground landscaped; private condominium (CapitaLand)
- Design: three bridging sky gardens as "interconnected streets, gardens and terraces in the air"; communal kitchens, event lawns; "vertical neighbourhood" descended from Habitat '67
- Source: https://www.capitaland.com/en/find-a-property/global-property-listing/residential/sky-habitat.html

### Choi Hung Estate — Hong Kong
- HK Housing Authority; 1963–64; one of HK's densest public estates (~6,730 residents/ha `[verify]`); heavily subsidised public rental
- Design: famous "rainbow" blocks; podium basketball court; open corridors and stair blocks as social space; ground-floor shops
- Note: subject of major heritage-save campaign (Save Choi Hung Estate)
- Source: https://www.savechoihungestate.com/story

### Trudo Vertical Forest — Eindhoven, Netherlands
- Architect: Stefano Boeri Architetti; 2021; 19 storeys; 70 apartments of ~50 m²; ~130 trees, 5,300 shrubs `[verify]`
- Model: **social housing** — world's first Vertical Forest as social housing; rent-capped for low/mid-income starters (rent ≈€710/month `[verify]`)
- Design: deep balconies with 1m+ greenery; microclimate and biodiversity benefits
- Sources: https://www.archdaily.com/976910/trudo-vertical-forest-stefano-boeri-architetti · https://www.stefanoboeriarchitetti.net/project/trudo-vertical-forest/

### Mirador (TP178) — Madrid, Spain
- Architect: MVRDV with Blanca Lleó; 2005; **156 dwellings** (146 social housing units `[verify]`) in ~18,300 m²; 21–22 storeys; client EMV (municipal housing company)
- Design: nine stacked "mini-neighbourhoods" with differentiated façades; semi-public **sky plaza at 40 m** with community garden; "vertical alleys"; red cores as vertical street sequence
- Sources: https://www.mvrdv.com/projects/135/mirador · https://arquitecturaviva.com/works/edificio-mirador-madrid-10

### Scheldezicht — Antwerp, Belgium
- Architect: C.F. Møller with BRUT; **completed 2020** (competition won 2014); 24-storey tower in Nieuw Zuid; private residential
- Design: explicitly a "vertical village" — shared zones and "mini-communities" stacked along façades; rooftop garden; entrance hall as meeting space; winter-garden balconies; Antwerp street culture translated into vertical social life
- Sources: https://www.cfmoller.com/g/Vertical-Villagers-are-settling-in-Antwerp-tower-Scheldezicht-i18101.html · https://archello.com/project/scheldezicht

### Tara Housing — New Delhi, India
- Architect: Raj Rewal Associates; 1978; ≈550 units; 6–7 storey stepped clusters; ≈2.8 ha (≈125 du/ha `[verify]`); cooperative/middle-income model
- Design: "streets in the sky" from the traditional *mohalla* — stepped pyramidal sections, pedestrian streets and courts, shaded plazas, tiered terraces, exposed brick, deep overhangs; public→semi-private→private hierarchy
- Note: nominated for Aga Khan Award `[verify cycle]`
- Source: https://tekton.mes.ac.in/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/Tekton-7.1-Practice-Sanjay.pdf

### Aranya Community Housing — Indore, India
- Architect: B.V. Doshi / Vastu-Shilpa Foundation; completed 1989; ≈6,500 units on ≈85–100 ha `[verify]`; incremental low-cost housing; services-first layout, "growing house" core + self-build
- Design: hierarchical "banyan tree" open-space system; community squares; central commercial spine; cross-subsidy of EWS/LIG by larger plots
- Award: **Aga Khan Award for Architecture 1995**; post-occupancy study by McGill MCHG
- Source: https://www.mcgill.ca/mchg/projects/post-occupancy-study-aranya-housing-project

### Bhendi Bazaar Redevelopment (Saifee Burhani Upliftment Project) — Mumbai, India
- Planner/Architects: **Mandviwala Qutub & Associates (MQA)** for Saifee Burhani Upliftment Trust; masterplan 2009–ongoing; **Phase 1 completed 2020** (two towers, 610 families + 128 businesses returned)
- Scale: 16.5 acres; 250+ buildings; 3,200 families, 1,250 shops rehoused in situ; ≈35,000 projected residents; replacement flats ≈350 sq ft vs ≈200 sq ft originals
- Model: in-situ cluster redevelopment by philanthropic trust — free rehousing with ownership transfer, cross-subsidised by sale of additional FSI; LEED Gold; no permanent displacement; phased relocation preserves social fabric; amenities floor + prayer hall per building
- Sources: https://sbut.com/ · ADB South Asia Working Paper 103: https://www.adb.org/sites/default/files/publication/1022101/sdwp-103-rejuvenating-cities-case-studies.pdf

### The Collective Old Oak — London, UK (co-living reference)
- Architect: PLP Architecture; 2016; ≈546–550 rooms; ~90,000 sq ft shared amenity `[verify]`; private co-living model — private bedrooms + shared kitchens, living rooms, workspaces, cinema, programming
- Source: PLP Architecture project page

### The Vertical Village (Taipei) — theoretical proposal
- MVRDV + The Why Factory (TU Delft), Winy Maas; nai010, 2011; manifesto-proposal stacking ~1,000 individual "houses" on shared mega-platforms at 200,000 units/km²; essential theoretical frame for the "village ≠ low-rise" argument
- Sources: https://www.mvrdv.com/projects/ · https://www.nai010.com/

## 2.2 Research Papers & Theses

### High-rise living: outcomes, wellbeing, community
1. **Yuen, B., Yeh, A., Appold, S.J., Earl, G., Ting, J., Kwee, L.K. (2006).** "High-rise living in Singapore public housing." *Urban Studies* 43(3): 583–600. DOI: 10.1080/00420980500533133. Density per se is not the problem; lifts, safety, cleanliness, services matter most.
2. **Gifford, R. (2007).** "The consequences of living in high-rise buildings." *Architectural Science Review* 50(1): 2–17. DOI: 10.3763/asre.2007.5002. Canonical review; findings inconsistent, contingent on design, floor, age, population.
3. **Bramley, G. & Power, S. (2009).** "Urban form and social sustainability." *Environment and Planning B* 36(1): 30–48. DOI: 10.1068/b33129. Housing *type* and services matter more than raw density.
4. **Kearns, A. et al. (2012).** Scottish high-rise vs other housing — high-rise residents fare worse on social indicators `[title/journal verify]`.
5. **Kalantari, S. & Shepley, M. (2021).** "Psychological and social impacts of high-rise buildings: a review of the POE literature." *Housing Studies* 36(10). DOI: 10.1080/02673037.2020.1752630. Shared spaces, corridors, greenery, management shape outcomes more than height.
6. **Nguyen, L. et al. (2020).** "Where do People Interact in High-Rise Apartment Buildings?" *IJERPH* 17(13): 4619. DOI: 10.3390/ijerph17134619. Hanoi, 274 residents: 46% of interactions occur in circulation areas not designed for them.
7. **Nguyen, L. et al. (2024).** "Social impacts of living in high-rise apartment buildings." *Journal of Urban Affairs*. DOI: 10.1080/07352166.2024.2311165. Neighbour networks → cohesion → attachment → less loneliness; communal space decisive.
8. **Mechlenborg, M. (2022).** "High Neighbor! Residents' Social Practices in New Danish High-Rises." *Urban Planning* 7(4): 339–351. DOI: 10.17645/up.v7i4.5629. Vertical social life starts horizontally at the front door; residents oscillate between "hotel" and "vertical village" ideals.
9. **Walsh, M., Arthurson, K. & Levin, I. (2021).** "Obtrusive Intimacy in Multi-Owned Housing." *Housing, Theory and Society* 38(5): 614–630. DOI: 10.1080/14036096.2021.1902386. Enforced proximity without community damages wellbeing.

## 2.4 Debates & Gaps

1. **Pruitt-Igoe myth:** tower failure blamed on form but driven by disinvestment/management/policy (Bristol 1991; Smith 2024)
2. **Density vs design:** density not the villain; type, shared space, services decisive (Bramley; Yuen) — but Kearns/Malik find high-rise lags; unresolved, context-dependent
3. **Mixed tenure ≠ social mix:** spatial integration doesn't produce interaction (Sautkina; Thurber; Arthurson) — vertical villages must engineer interaction
4. **"Kampung spirit" critique:** state marketing vs residents' avoidance of neighbourly ties (Wee; Chong; Lim)
5. **Appropriation vs governance:** corridor personalisation predicts interaction (Gao; Yang 2025) but fire/management regimes criminalise it
6. **Cultural contingency:** East Asian acceptance vs Western resistance (Yuen; Forrest; Mechlenborg); typology transfer (Singapore→India) needs cultural adaptation
7. **Gaps:** no POE of vertical-village typologies (Kampung Admiralty, Mirador, Trudo); place-keeping of sky gardens under-theorised; children/older adults/disability in towers under-studied; vertical economies (markets, food-growing at height) unexamined; India: no POE of PMAY towers/Light House Projects, Bhendi vs Dharavi comparison underexplored; comparative finance (HDB/Vienna/Bhendi cross-subsidy → PMAY) lacking

## 3.4 Debates, Gaps & Dissertation Angles

1. **Objects vs skills:** UNESCO frames craft heritage as *skills*, challenging the museum's object-based paradigm — a strong theoretical hook for "interpretation centre" (workshop-led) vs "museum" (display-led)
2. **Commodification vs authenticity:** Cohen (1988) → Phuket 2026 paper; does touristic craft production freeze or sustain traditions?
3. **Living vs frozen heritage:** process-led interpretation (Khamir, LLDC, Anokhi) vs display-led; "safeguarding without freezing"
4. **Economic vs cultural value:** policy measured in exports/income (SFURTI +15–18%; ₹33,122 cr exports; 64–71% women) — museums rarely evaluated on cultural outcomes
5. **New build vs adaptive reuse:** Kanazawa (relocated heritage), Anokhi (haveli), NCM Delhi (purpose-built), Khamir (village-like new-build), Champawat (material-driven) — different arguments for what the architecture itself "interprets"
6. **Gaps:** no visitor/impact studies for Indian craft centres; craft settlements' built heritage under-documented (Bagchee); ecomuseum model unexplored in Indian craft context (Kutch/Chanderi); material/regional identity as interpretive medium treated descriptively; Japan's mingei/kogei and Mexico's Oaxaca models rarely cross-referenced with Indian literature

# Topic 4 — Active Riverfront: Resilient Public Recreational Hub

## 4.1 Case Studies (10 built precedents)

### Cheonggyecheon Stream Restoration — Seoul, South Korea
- Designers: Seoul Metropolitan Government; SeoAhn Total Landscape (landscape); 2003–2005; ~100 acres, 5.84 km; ~$380M total (~$120M landscape)
- Program: 22 new bridges; continuous pedestrian/bike green corridor; wetlands; terraced flood walls; 29 habitat zones
- Resilience: 200-year flood protection, sustains 118 mm/hr flow; water pumped (120,000 tons/day from Han River + subway pumps) to maintain 40 cm depth
- Outcomes: biodiversity +639% (2003–08); air pollution −35%; corridor 3.3–5.9°C cooler than parallel road; ~64,000 visitors/day; land prices +30–50% within 50 m; catalyzed ~$1.98B redevelopment; 330M cumulative visitors in 20 years (~47,000/day avg)
- Sources: https://www.landscapeperformance.org/case-study-briefs/cheonggyecheon-stream-restoration-project · https://udcsa.gsd.harvard.edu/projects/9

### Sabarmati Riverfront Development Project — Ahmedabad, India
- Client: Sabarmati Riverfront Development Corporation (SRFDCL) `[verify]`; masterplan consultants incl. HOK `[verify]`; initiated 2005; ~13 km reclaimed
- Program: continuous promenades, parks, gardens, walkways; pedestrian-only bridge; annual flower show
- Resilience: channelization and embankment walls for flood control (criticised as hard engineering)
- Community: socio-economic gains for some; documented displacement/weak resettlement of riverbank slum dwellers
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabarmati_Riverfront · https://www.jstor.org/stable/23065609 · https://repository.nls.ac.in/slr/vol12/iss2/7/

### Buffalo Bayou Park — Houston, USA
- Designers: SWA Group (masterplan), Page Architects (structures), Reed Hilderbrand (horticulture); opened 2015; 169 acres, 2.3 miles; $58M public-private partnership
- Program: 15+ miles of trails, event lawns, amphitheaters, dog park, skate park, Water Works cistern venue, bat colony overlook
- Resilience: channel sinuosity restored (fluvial geomorphology + HEC modeling); hydrodynamic bridge columns; vegetated 2:1 banks ($72.50/lf vs $925.80/lf gabion); flood cut-off light poles; lowest path 12 ft above water

- Sources: https://www.landscapeperformance.org/case-study-briefs/buffalo-bayou-park · https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13504509.2020.1870249

### Bishan–Ang Mo Kio Park / Kallang River — Singapore

## 4.4 Debates & Gaps

1. **Ecological authenticity vs engineered spectacle:** Cheonggyecheon's flow is pumped (120,000 t/day) — is a "restored" river with artificial flow a valid model? Conflicting verdicts in systematic review vs ex-post CBA; Isar shows same tension in Europe
2. **Equity, displacement, green gentrification:** Sabarmati slum eviction critiques (JSTOR 23065609; NLS article); Cheonggyecheon land +30–50%; waterfront renewal as elite capture (2022 *Journal of Urbanism*); Global South needs displacement-mitigation protocols
3. **Flood resilience vs recreational continuity:** Buffalo Bayou post-Harvey failures (slope failures, 68M lbs sediment, under-designed drainage) — resilience is a continuous operational process; maintenance budgets must be part of the design argument
4. **Data gaps:** post-occupancy monitoring rare; biodiversity and visitor metrics rarely linked (I-SEER index; MDPI dual-benefit study are first steps); climate-change flood projections seldom integrated into riverfront master plans
5. **India-specific gaps:** sacred/ritual riverfronts (Varanasi ghats) vs modern recreational programs; monsoon hydrology under-studied; NIUA's URM Index offers a transferable framework; NMCG data is program-level, not design-level
6. **Suggested dissertation angle:** integrated framework — floodable bioengineered edges (Buffalo Bayou/Bishan model) + operational maintenance/flood-response strategy (Buffalo Bayou lessons) + displacement-aware phasing (Sabarmati counter-case) — tested on an Indian monsoon riverfront
